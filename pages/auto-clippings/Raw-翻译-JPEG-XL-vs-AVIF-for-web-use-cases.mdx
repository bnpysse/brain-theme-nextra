JPEG XL vs AVIF for web use-cases

JPEG XL has now landed in all major browsers, so I figured I'd take a closer look at what it's good/bad at compared to other formats.

Conclusions

I'll go into full detail, with examples and benchmarks, but for those of you who don't have the time, here are the headlines:

For lossy photos on the web, AVIF almost always produces smaller files than JPEG XL. At default effort settings, the best JPEG XL version is typically 15â25% larger than the equivalent AVIF. At maximum effort settings, the gap narrows a little, to typically 6â20%. There are outliers, where JPEG XL is close to, or even beats AVIF, so if you want the absolute best result for a particular image, it's worth trying both. See the F1 image for a typical example, and the weasel for an outlier.

The JPEG XL encoder matters a lot. jixel, a newer JPEG XL encoder, usually produces smaller files than libjxl, except when the image has detailed transparency. The people image is one example.

For screenshots and illustrations, AVIF is much better than JPEG XL. The equivalent JPEG XL is 30â90% larger at default effort settings, and 20â70% larger at maximum effort settings. Lossy AVIF handles these well - you don't need to use lossless formats unless you really need bit-exact results. See the screenshot and illustration examples.

For images with transparency, AVIF is much better than JPEG XL. The libjxl version is 31â44% larger at default effort settings, and 61â70% larger at maximum effort settings. jixel always encodes transparency losslessly, which makes its files 2â3x the size of AVIF's. See the data.

AVIF is generally faster to encode, and uses less memory, when you factor in file size. jixel is faster than AVIF and uses less memory, but its files are typically significantly larger, so AVIF is better on balance. See the F1 image for one example.

AVIF is faster to decode. JPEG XL typically takes 2â3x as long to decode at medium quality, but the gap narrows at very high qualities, where Chrome can sometimes decode JPEG XL faster. The people image is one example.

Higher effort settings are worth it for AVIF and jixel, but not libjxl. libjxl's higher efforts are much slower, use a lot more memory, and barely improve the result. Sometimes they produce larger files than the default effort. The screenshot is an example of this.

AVIF's 4:2:0 mode (half colour resolution) shouldn't be your default. It rarely reduces file size, and it makes higher qualities harder to reach. Although, it is quicker to encode and decode. Stick with 4:4:4 if you don't have time to check both. The F1 image is one example, and the screenshot is an exception.

Quality settings are reliable for all the encoders. With a single quality setting, the resulting quality varies a bit between images, but all the encoders vary in the same direction, and in ways that seem reasonable. See the data.

Progressive JPEG XL is basically free at high effort. jixel recently added support for early progressive previews, and at its slowest setting they cost little or nothing, but at its default setting the files are 12% bigger. Firefox and Chrome support progressive JPEG XL. Safari still renders the final image, but doesn't show progressive previews. However, is this type of progressive rendering a good user experience? See the examples and data.

AVIF supports progressive rendering! It's Chrome-only for now, but it's in progress for Firefox. It's more complex than progressive JPEG XL, but it gives you more control over what the preview looks like, the files are usually smaller, and they're much faster to decode. See for yourself.

For HDR, JPEG XL lacks gain maps, which is a significant limitation compared to AVIF. The JPEG XL format supports gain maps, but they aren't supported by browser decoders, and the encoding story is messy/experimental. When comparing non-gain mapped HDR content, the two quality metrics I used disagree a lot, but they agree that JPEG XL produces smaller files compared to AVIF. See the examples.

JPEG XL can make existing JPEGs 9â20% smaller, without further loss. It's also as fast or faster to decode, except for small images. See the data.

For lossless, JPEG XL is the clear winner. Lossless WebP is 9â17% larger for photos, and lossless AVIF is 30â120% larger. But lossless images are almost never what you want on the web. If you do use them on the web (like I do on this page), watch out for jixel's higher efforts - it often produces files that are too slow to decode. See the data.

Animated image formats continue to be a dumb idea. Please don't use them. Learn more, if you must.

For web use-cases, I'd currently default to AVIF 4:4:4, with the highest effort level I can be bothered with. If I felt AVIF was producing an unusually large file, I'd take a look at JPEG XL via jixel, and see if it did better.

Both AVIF and JPEG XL (via jixel) have seen massive improvements recently, so it's possible that this recommendation could change in future. There's been a lot of misinformation around this stuff, and your AI will happily deliver that misinformation to you. Instead, test things with your own images.

AVIF folks suggest that JPEG XL is technically limited compared to AVIF. That might be true, but in this article I'm focusing on results with today's implementations.

Methodology

I 'created' a benchmarking script, that encodes images using various codecs at various settings, and records various details.

I'm using the latest released versions of the image codecs, which at time of writing is:

- avifenc 1.4.2

- libjxl 0.12.0 (using its cjxl encoder)

- jixel 0.4.2 (git f487299 ), a second JPEG XL encoder

- cwebp 1.6.0 (lossless WebP is used later in the article)

I chose a selection of images, and encoded most of them to a width of 1598 pixels - which is 2x the maximum width images appear on this blog. This means they look sharp on high density displays, which most users have. As a result, if you're viewing this on a small or low density screen, you're not getting the full experience.

I ran these benchmarks on my 2024 MacBook Pro (M4 Pro).

Lossy - F1 image

When I first wrote about AVIF, I used an F1 car as the first image, so I figured I'd do that again!

I encoded the image at various codec qualities, and chose versions that were closest to particular SSIMULACRA2 scores - a perceptual image quality metric by the folks behind JPEG XL. 50 is considered medium quality, 70 high, 80 very high, and 90 visually lossless. Although, I'm not sure if these ratings take high density displays into account.

Throughout the article, the visual comparisons, like above, use each encoder's maximum effort, except in cases where maximum effort results in a larger file (yes, that happens).

For archival, I'd tend towards near-lossless qualities, but if I'm just displaying an illustrative image on my blog, performance is more important to me. I'm personally happy with the 70 result above. If you zoom in, you can see the quality loss, but if I was really concerned about the zoom-in case, I'd use something like the VisualViewport API to load in a higher resolution image when that happens, rather than make the download bigger for everyone.

At lower qualities you can see the difference in strategy between the codecs. The detail loss is around the same, but AVIF tends to smooth things out compared to the original, whereas JPEG XL seems to introduce rougher patterns.

Also, I feel that, at medium quality (50), the AVIF and libjxl look better than the jixel version. The jixel version has a lot of visible noise around the BWT logo, and the Microsoft text on the front wing is way less legible. It makes me wonder about the metric a bit.

Anyway, here's my interpretation of the results:

- File size: AVIF wins. At default efforts, the best JPEG XL image is 14â17% larger than the AVIF image for the same quality. jixel is the better JPEG XL encoder at medium/high quality, and libjxl is slightly better at very high quality. At maximum efforts, the best JPEG XL image (jixel 'extraslow') is 6â15% larger than AVIF (s0).

- jixel at its slowest setting slightly beats AVIF's default effort, with files 1â10% smaller. The biggest gain is at very high quality.

- Encode time: AVIF wins. At default efforts, jixel is 12â36% faster than AVIF, but its files are 14â18% larger. To match the file size of AVIF's default, jixel needs its 'extraslow' setting, which takes 18â27x as long as AVIF. libjxl's default takes 1.5â2x as long as AVIF's default, and still produces bigger files.

- Encode memory: AVIF wins. At default efforts, jixel uses around half the memory of AVIF, but again, its files are 14â18% larger. To match the file size of AVIF's default, jixel 'extraslow' uses ~10x the memory. libjxl's default uses 2â3x the memory of AVIF, and its higher efforts use ~7x.

- Decode time: AVIF wins. JPEG XL takes 2â3x as long to decode at medium quality, narrowing to ~1.5â1.8x at very high quality. Safari's JPEG XL decoder is the slowest.

- Higher effort is worth it for AVIF. AVIF's s5 gets you ~10% smaller files for ~3x the encode time.

- Higher effort is worth it for jixel too, up to 'slow'. 'slow' gets you 9â13% smaller files than its default 'fast', for ~3.7x the encode time. 'extraslow' saves another 3â8%, but takes ~8x longer than 'slow' and uses ~9x the memory. Even so, AVIF still wins when given the same encode time: AVIF s5 takes about as long as jixel 'slow', and its files are ~13% smaller.

- Higher effort isn't worth it for libjxl. libjxl's maximum effort is barely any better than its default, but takes ~18x longer.

- AVIF's 4:2:0 mode doesn't help much. It's slightly faster to encode/decode, and uses less memory, but the files aren't meaningfully smaller, and it struggles to reach higher qualities.

Encoding performance

When it comes to images on my blog, I encode with maximum effort settings. I'm happy to wait a couple of seconds to get an image that's faster to load for every user. However, if you're doing a lot of encoding on a server, encode time becomes more of a concern.

Lossy - people

I've heard people say that AVIF's tendency to smooth out details makes it bad for human faces, so I picked this image from Unsplash.

The results of the benchmark for this image:

- File size: AVIF wins. At default efforts, the best JPEG XL image is 17â24% larger than the AVIF image for the same quality. jixel is the better JPEG XL encoder across the whole quality range this time. libjxl is 30â50% larger than AVIF, which is a much bigger gap than the F1 image. At maximum efforts, the best JPEG XL image (jixel 'extraslow') is 12â17% larger than AVIF (s0).

- jixel at its slowest setting doesn't quite match AVIF's default effort this time. It's a few percent larger at medium/high quality, and only edges ahead at very high quality.

- Encode time: AVIF wins. At default efforts, jixel is 18â33% faster than AVIF, but its files are 17â24% larger. - Encode memory: AVIF wins. At default efforts, jixel uses around half the memory of AVIF, but again, its files are 17â24% larger. jixel 'extraslow' gets closest to AVIF's default file size, but uses ~10x the memory. libjxl's default uses 2â3.3x the memory of AVIF, and its higher efforts use ~7x.

- Higher effort is worth it for AVIF. AVIF's s5 gets you 5â8% smaller files for ~3x the encode time. That's a smaller gain than the F1 image.

- Higher effort is somewhat worth it for libjxl on this image. Unlike the F1 image, libjxl's maximum effort gets you 4â11% smaller files than its default. But it takes ~10x longer, and it still produces much bigger files than AVIF at its default effort.

- AVIF's 4:2:0 mode doesn't help much. Same as the F1 image. ColorVideoVDP gives it a small (~4%) advantage, but SSIMULACRA2 doesn't.

At lower qualities, you can see the smoothing on the face, but it's the same for both AVIF and JPEG XL.

The results of the benchmark:

- File size: AVIF wins, by a lot. At default efforts, the best JPEG XL image is 73â89% larger than the AVIF image at medium/high quality (jixel), and 29â44% larger at very high quality (libjxl). At maximum efforts, the best JPEG XL image (jixel 'extraslow' with patches) is 23â73% larger than AVIF (s0).

- Encode time: AVIF wins. At default efforts, jixel is ~50% faster than AVIF, but its files are 70â98% larger. libjxl's default takes 1.7â1.8x as long as AVIF's default, and still produces much bigger files.

- Encode memory: AVIF wins. At default efforts, jixel uses ~40% less memory than AVIF, but again, its files are much larger. libjxl's default uses 1.2â2.2x the memory of AVIF. jixel 'extraslow' with patches gets closest to AVIF's file sizes, but uses ~7.5x the memory of AVIF's default.

- Decode time: AVIF wins, by a lot. Taking the faster of the two JPEG XL encoders' output, JPEG XL takes 2â3.8x as long to decode, depending on browser and quality.

- Higher effort is more worth it for AVIF on this image. AVIF's s5 gets you 6â13% smaller files for 2.5â3x the encode time, and s0 gets you 16â23% smaller files for 30â45x the encode time.

- Higher effort is worth it for jixel, as long as you enable patches. 'medium' with patches gets you 11â44% smaller files than its default 'fast', for 3â4x the encode time. 'slow' with patches saves another 7â16% for ~1.7x the time of 'medium'. 'extraslow' with patches saves another few percent, but takes ~5x longer than 'slow' and uses ~4.5x the memory.

- AVIF's 4:2:0 mode helps at higher qualities. At SSIMULACRA2 70 and above, it produces files 7â18% smaller than 4:4:4. At lower qualities, it makes no difference.

- All three encoders produce higher SSIMULACRA2 scores for this image. Opposite to the weasel image. But, this seems correct. At SSIMULACRA2 70 the artifacts are pretty noticeable around the Firefox logo (to me, anyway), so I'd go with 80, which is where the codecs lean anyway.

Some developers, especially those who've been around as long as I have, seem to think things like screenshots need to be delivered in a lossless format. But I think this is because they're used to formats like JPEG and WebP (and seemingly JPEG XL) which handle this kind of image badly, whereas lossy AVIF handles them really well.
