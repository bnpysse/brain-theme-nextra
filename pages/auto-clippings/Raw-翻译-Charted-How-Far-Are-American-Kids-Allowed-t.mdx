How Much Freedom Do American Kids Have?

Key Takeaways

Even at age 17, most American teens still face some limit on how far they can go alone.

At age 10, nearly three in five children cannot go beyond their yard without an adult.

62% of parents say children ages 8 to 12 should receive more supervision than they currently do.

How much freedom do American children have to move around on their own?

This visualization tracks how those boundaries change with age, from being required to stay inside the house to being allowed to go anywhere without adult supervision.

The data comes from the

Institute for Family Studies

, based on its 2025 Survey of American Parenting Culture. The survey included nearly 24,000 U.S. parents representing more than 40,000 children under 18, with teenagers also surveyed directly in some households.

Freedom Expands With Age

For younger children, staying close to home is the norm. At age four, about 71% are not allowed to leave the house without an adult.

Those boundaries gradually expand as children move through elementary school.

By age 10, most children still have fairly tight limits. Roughly 58% cannot go beyond their yard alone, while unrestricted freedom remains rare.

A more noticeable shift begins in the early teenage years. Children are increasingly allowed to move around their neighborhoods on their own, although relatively few have complete freedom to travel wherever they like.

Even Older Teens Face Limits

Independence increases much faster during the later

teenage years

.

By age 17, about four in 10 teens can go anywhere they like without an adult. That still leaves a majority facing some geographic limit on where they can go alone.

For some teens, those limits remain substantial. The survey found that 8% of 17-year-olds are not allowed to leave the house without an adult.

Most Parents Favor More Supervision

According to the survey, 62% of parents believe children ages 8 to 12 should receive more supervision than they currently do.

Researchers also examined whether neighborhood design might help explain differences in children’s independence. Among respondents who could be matched with geographic data, they found no correlation between neighborhood walkability and children’s autonomy.

Physical Freedom vs. Digital Access

The IFS report places children’s physical independence alongside another major part of modern childhood: their growing access to the digital world.

While the freedom to travel alone expands gradually with age, time spent on

internet-connected devices

rises much more quickly. By age 17, children average nearly 20 hours per week on connected devices.

Unsupervised outdoor time remains far lower. Even at 17, the average is just 2.4 hours per week.

The contrast highlights one of the report’s broader findings: as children get older, their digital world opens up much faster than their freedom to navigate the physical world on their own.
