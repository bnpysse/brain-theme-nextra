How America’s Trade Deficit Shifted From Oil to AI

Key Takeaways

The U.S. trade deficit in semiconductors and AI hardware reached an annualized $546 billion in the second quarter of 2026, more than double its level a year earlier.

That surpassed both the machinery and transport equipment deficit ($451 billion) and the oil deficit’s 2008 peak ($414 billion).

Over the past 35 years, America’s trade deficit has undergone three major shifts, from oil and petroleum to machinery and transport equipment, and now the chips and servers powering artificial intelligence.

The latest wave has grown at an extraordinary pace. Since 2020, the U.S. trade deficit in semiconductors and AI hardware has increased more than ninefold, reflecting surging demand for imported computing equipment.

Semiconductors and AI Hardware Are the Third Wave

The deficit in semiconductors and AI hardware barely registered before 2010, but the category has since become a major driver of America’s goods trade imbalance.

Imports are driving the widening gap. In the first seven months of 2026, the U.S. imported $429 billion of semiconductors and AI hardware, compared with $132 billion in exports.

America’s AI infrastructure boom is a major driver of this shift. As technology companies build increasingly powerful data centers, demand for imported servers, chips, and computing equipment has surged.

According to the

BEA

, U.S. imports of computers increased by $101.4 billion in 2025, while imports of computer accessories rose by another $42.7 billion amid the

AI buildout

. The broader hardware category also includes equipment used outside AI applications.

Much of this hardware has also avoided tariffs. Computers and chips were exempted from reciprocal tariffs in April 2025, while a 2026 tariff on certain advanced chips

does not apply

to those imported for use in U.S. data centers.

Oil’s Deficit Disappeared as U.S. Production Boomed

The first wave of America’s oil deficit peaked at an annualized $414 billion in the third quarter of 2008, when oil prices spiked. Shale drilling then transformed U.S. production, and in 2020 the country became a net

petroleum exporter

for the first time since at least 1949.

By 2024, the oil deficit had disappeared from the chart, reflecting America’s declining

dependence on OPEC oil

. Machinery and transport equipment had already become the second wave, with its deficit reaching a peak of $575 billion in late 2024.

As a share of GDP, the machinery deficit has remained near 1.5% since the early 2000s, growing roughly in step with the economy.

While the semiconductor and AI hardware deficit has surpassed oil’s historic peak in dollar terms, the picture changes when adjusted for the size of the economy.

The latest deficit represents 1.7% of U.S. GDP, compared with 2.8% at the height of the oil deficit in 2008. America’s newest trade imbalance is larger in dollars, but oil’s peak was greater relative to the size of the economy.
