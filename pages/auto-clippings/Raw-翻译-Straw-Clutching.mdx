【文章标题】：Straw Clutching

【文章正文】：

“Virtue is more to be feared than vice, because its excesses are not subject to the regulation of conscience.”

- Adam Smith

A classic trick in debate is distorting your opponent’s position, then nullifying your reframing of their view. This straw man tactic is particularly effective when the semantic maneuvering is sly and subtle, hence its permeation throughout political discourse.

Consider the diesel crisis facing US President Donald Trump as he hurtles toward an ominous midterm election. Trump has many levers with which to restrain runaway domestic prices for that all-important fuel, and we have always been careful in our explanation of them. “Toggle” and “limit” are among the verbs we have chosen to describe his options on exports, for example. We have purposefully avoided the word “ban,” as such a drastic measure would unleash unintended consequences, including, possibly, making the problem worse.

Up against it | Getty

There’s no room for subtlety when the cash register is ringing, and our friends in industry can be forgiven for wanting the party to continue just a bit longer. To that end, a powerful set of lobbyists and allies has worked to frame Trump’s choice as binary, either “ban” or “as you were,” and an avalanche of criticism has thundered through the media to nullify the former. Here’s an

example editorial

from

The Wall Street Journal

:

“

Faced with record high diesel prices, some lawmakers are calling for a ban on US diesel exports. The argument sounds simple: If diesel is scarce, stop sending American-made fuel overseas and build up the supply at home. That misidentifies the problem…

…the diesel shortage isn’t a result of the US exporting too much fuel. It is a global refining crisis. US refiners are supplying American consumers while cushioning a volatile global market that is driving prices higher at home.

”

The fallacious implication, of course, is that US refiners have an inherent right—nay, an obligation—to participate in said global markets. For all the lip service paid to the ideal of free-market capitalism, every refinery in the world operates with the express consent of the government where the asset resides, including in America. That consent is always conditional, and the US government is currently led by President Trump.

That we were not the only ones aware of the game being played became clear last week, when industry veteran and current US Energy Secretary Chris Wright began spreading what amounted to a you-can-still-do-it-the-easy-way

message

:

“

US Energy Secretary Chris Wright has contacted executives at several major American refiners in recent days to gauge support for a voluntary restriction on diesel exports as the Trump administration searches for ​an alternative to a short-term ban, according to three people familiar with the discussions.

The effort to enlist companies voluntarily underscores the limited options available to the White ‌House amid surging fuel prices that are pushing inflation higher and beginning to ripple through the broader economy as crucial November midterm elections approach.

”

An offer you shouldn’t refuse | Getty

Having observed many such tussles between industry and Washington over the decades, we put pen to paper today to argue that the “limited options available” framing is little more than wishcasting on the part of those with a vested financial interest in elevated diesel prices. Like it or lump it, Trump has many cards to play to get his way ahead of the elections, and if he doesn’t succeed, life looks likely to get rather complicated for an industry currently riding high. It would be value-maximizing for industry players to play ball with him. Let’s explore why.
