It seems you're grappling with a nuanced discussion about power, influence, and the role of ideas in shaping societal outcomes. Let's break down some key points:

### Acemoglu and Johnson on Power and Persuasion

1. **Randomness vs. Systematic Influence**:
   - **Acemoglu and Johnson acknowledge that the spread of ideas is partly random**: The success or failure of an idea can depend on luck, timing, and other stochastic factors.
   - **However, they also argue for systematic influences**: Social status, institutional support, and existing hierarchies play crucial roles in determining which ideas prevail.

2. **Persuasion as Power**:
   - They define "power" broadly to include not just compulsion but also persuasion. They suggest that influential individuals and institutions can shape public opinion through their persuasive capabilities.
   - For instance, the influence of tech-industrialists on policy-making is seen as a form of power, even if it's often indirect or random.

3. **Intrinsic Persuasiveness**:
   - Acemoglu and Johnson believe that some ideas are more inherently compelling due to their simplicity, story-telling quality, and apparent truth.
   - This intrinsic persuasiveness can be seen as a form of power, as it allows an individual or group to influence others without relying solely on compulsion.

### Noah's Critique

1. **Power Defined Too Broadly**:
   - You argue that defining power so broadly (including both compulsion and intrinsic persuasiveness) dilutes the concept.
   - Specifically, you find it problematic when ideas themselves are considered a form of power because this definition seems to conflate distinct concepts.

2. **Consumer Choice vs. Persuasion**:
   - The concern is that defining power in such a way might delegitimize consumer choice and instead attribute broad popularity to coercion or manipulation.
   - This approach could be seen as an effort to delegitimate the success of companies like Apple, Facebook, Amazon, etc., by framing it as illegitimate influence rather than user-driven preference.

### Henry's Counterpoints

1. **Social Influence as Power**:
   - Henry argues that social influence is real and can be measured in terms of the number of people influenced.
   - He points out that if someone like Noah Smith has more subscribers, he likely exerts more power (influence) on average.

2. **Tautological Definition**:
   - You argue against a definition where "power" becomes tautological: having power means influencing others; and influencing others means having power.
   - This circularity is seen as unfalsifiable and practically useless in understanding real-world dynamics.

### Balancing the Discussion

1. **Separating Compulsion and Persuasion**:
   - It might be more productive to keep compulsion (coercion) and persuasion (influence through ideas and social status) as distinct concepts.
   - Acknowledging both can provide a richer understanding of how societal outcomes are shaped.

2. **Contextualizing Power**:
   - Recognize that power can operate at multiple levels: institutional, individual, ideological, etc.
   - Understanding these different forms of power can help in formulating effective strategies to address inequalities and injustices.

### Conclusion

While your critique highlights valid concerns about the broad definition of "power," Acemoglu and Johnson's approach offers a broader perspective on how ideas and institutions shape societies. Balancing these perspectives could lead to a more nuanced understanding of societal dynamics. The key is to ensure that definitions remain useful tools for analysis rather than becoming so broad as to lose their practical application.
