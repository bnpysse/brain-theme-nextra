How the Balance Between Men and Women Varies Worldwide

Key Takeaways:

Qatar has the world’s most male-skewed population, with nearly 246 men for every 100 women, largely due to its migrant workforce.

Moldova and Russia are among the most female-skewed countries, with fewer than 87 men per 100 women.

Europe and much of Latin America have female-majority populations, while South Asia, parts of East Asia, and the Middle East generally skew male.

Women generally live longer than men, but that doesn’t mean every country has more women than men. In some countries, men outnumber women by more than two to one.

This visualization by

Iswardi Ishak

, using 2025 data from the

United Nations Population Division Data Portal

, maps the number of males per 100 females worldwide. A ratio above 100 indicates more males, while a figure below 100 indicates more females.

The scale of these differences is striking. Qatar has 245.9 males per 100 females, followed by the UAE (176.0) and Oman (166.0).

At the other extreme, Hong Kong has just 81.8 males per 100 females, followed by Guadeloupe (82.4) and Martinique (82.9).

Why Do Eastern Europe and Russia Have More Women?

Across Eastern Europe and former Soviet countries, women’s longer life expectancy is amplified by elevated male mortality. Cardiovascular disease, alcohol-related deaths, and historical demographic shocks have all contributed to the imbalance.

Pew Research Center

also highlights the lasting demographic consequences of World War II and differences in male and female survival rates.

The imbalance is particularly pronounced among older age groups, where women substantially outnumber men. In countries such as Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia, decades of lower male life expectancy have contributed to some of the world’s most female-skewed populations.

Why Are Gulf Countries So Male-Dominated?

The Persian Gulf presents the opposite pattern. Qatar, the UAE, Kuwait, and Saudi Arabia have exceptionally male-heavy populations because they attract large numbers of

predominantly male

migrant workers, particularly in construction and infrastructure.

Many of these workers arrive from South Asian countries such as India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, and Nepal, often without their families. This creates an unusually large population of working-age men, pushing national gender ratios far beyond what natural birth patterns would produce.

Elsewhere in Asia, male-skewed populations in countries such as India and China reflect different forces, including historical son preference and sex-selective births. Unlike the Gulf states, these imbalances are driven more by long-term demographic and cultural factors than labor migration.
