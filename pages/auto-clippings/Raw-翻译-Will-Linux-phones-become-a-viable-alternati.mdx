【文章标题】：Will Linux phones become a viable alternative to the Android/iOS duopoly?

【文章正文】：

by Amos Batto (2026-10-05), License: CC BY 4.0, Download: PDF, ODT

Five years ago, Linux phones looked like they were poised to become a viable alternative to the Android/iOS duopoly that has an iron grip on the mobile phone industry. Sadly, after a brief flash of activity and community engagement with the release of the Purism Librem 5 and the PINE64 PinePhone in 2020, the Linux phone market has generally stagnated. This stagnation occurred partly because of the problems at Purism and PINE64, but also because the available Linux SoC’s weren’t up to the task of running a modern smartphone, and the hardware industry wasn’t providing any good alternatives with good free/open source drivers. In addition, Plasma Mobile and Phosh were still inchoate mobile interfaces at the time, and there wasn’t a good distro option to run these interfaces on Android kernels with a normal integrated mobile SoC. The poor performance of the PinePhone and Librem 5 and the problems with the PinePhone Pro and the Librem 5 refunds sapped people’s enthusiasm and willingness to take a chance on the next Linux phone.

Fortunately, all of these conditions are changing, and I believe that there is now the possibility that Linux could finally become a viable alternative to Google’s Android and Apple’s iOS in the mobile space, just like desktop Linux has become a viable alternative to Windows and Mac OSX on the PC over the last three decades. This isn’t to say that mobile Linux will fundamentally challenge the business of Google or Apple, but the conditions are ripe for a small market to arise for Linux phones, similar to the way that people can currently buy Linux PCs from a number of small companies, such as TUXEDO, System76, Purism, Slimbook, ThinkPenguin, Ubuntushop.biz, Entroware, Star Labs, Juno, Laptop with Linux, Linux Certified, ArkPC, etc.

It isn’t impossible that one of the big phone brands might sell a few models of phones with mobile Linux in the future, just like Lenovo and Dell currently sell a few PC models with Linux preinstalled in some limited markets. Motorola just announced that it will start selling some high-end phone models with GrapheneOS in 2027, which indicates that Google will allow the big phone makers to sell alternatives, probably because it fears anti-trust regulation. As the mobile industry increasingly shuts down the ability to unlock the bootloaders of Android phones, more people will be looking for an alternative to AOSP derivatives, and mobile Linux could provide that alternative.

Five years ago, it looked like mobile Linux had finally arrived. The Purism Librem 5 and PINE64 PinePhone had just been released, and even a big tech reviewer like Linus Tech Tips was trying them out. Mrwhosetheboss’s video, “Are Linux Smartphones about to KILL Android?,” where he held up the Librem 5, got 7.8 million views on YouTube. The Librem 5 had been ported to Mobian and postmarketOS, and the PinePhone had been ported to 20 different Linux distributions. There was a growing number of phone models that could run Jolla’s Sailfish OS and Ubuntu Touch on top of Android kernels, and many people were testing postmarketOS on their old phones. Plasma Mobile and Phosh were advancing fast as mobile interfaces. KDE had adopted Kirigami and GNOME was adopting Adwaita, so both KDE and GNOME apps could become adaptive between desktop and mobile interfaces. The Waydroid project was making it possible to run Android apps inside a Linux container. The future seemed bright for mobile Linux, but that future never seemed to arrive. It is worth examining what held back mobile Linux to understand how those conditions can be overcome in the future.

There has been great deal of experimentation with different mobile operating systems based on a Linux kernel over the last 25 years since the first mobile Linux devices were released—the Sharp Zaurus, the G.Mate Yopy and the Motorola A760. I count 29 different varieties of commercial mobile Linux that have existed over the years, and there are probably many more that I missed. There have been many different waves of mobile Linux, which at the time looked like they might become the next big thing, including EZX, MotoMagx, Maemo, LiMo, Moblin, Meego, Tizen, Firefox OS, webOS, Ubuntu Touch and Sailfish OS. For years Motorola experimented with different Linux varieties that were based on the idea of using Java apps available in a community store, so Google’s Android was hardly unique, but it was the flavor of mobile Linux which succeeded. Today, all of them are often lumped together under the label of “Linux” as a separate category from Android, but Tizen and Firefox OS are just as distinct architecturally as Android from a standard desktop Linux distro.

The mobile Linux operating systems live on, although they are less visible today. Much of the MeeGo code that Nokia created for the N9, continues to be developed by the tiny Finnish company, the Jolla Group, which is once again selling phones with its Sailfish OS. Firefox OS, which was abandoned by Mozilla Foundation in 2016, was reborn in 2017 as proprietary KaiOS for low-end phones without touch screens, but that market is slowly disappearing as Android now reaches even the cheapest segments of the mobile phone market. Ubuntu Touch, which was abandoned by Canonical in 2017, lives on as a community-based project under the volunteers at UBports. Even, webOS and Tizen still exist, although they are no longer used as mobile operating systems. webOS runs LG TVs and Tizen is used in Samsung TVs.

Although it doesn’t generate many headlines in the tech press, a tiny Linux phone market still exists. Purism, PINE64, FuriLabs, Jolla, Jolla-Devices, Volla and PrivacyPortal (plus Brax in the future) all sell phones with some flavor of Linux preinstalled. The number of people buying Linux phones today is fewer than in 2020-22 when PINE64 was launching the PinePhone and PinePhone Pro, but there are actually more companies selling Linux phones than there was 5 years ago. There are good reasons to believe that many of the problems that plagued Linux phones in the past are going to be overcome in the future.

The SoC dilemma: weak but long lived vs powerful but short lived

In the past, the available hardware choices limited the viability of Linux phones. Systems-on-a-Chip (SoC’s) which support Linux are generally used in single-board-computers (SBC’s), TV setup boxes, internet of things (IoT) devices and embedded microprocessors in industrial and automotive applications, where the price points are lower, so their performance is generally weaker and they are less energy efficient than the typical mobile SoC designed for smartphones, because they are fabbed on older process nodes. However, they offer long-term support, because they are used in applications that have long product life cycles. For example, automobiles contain dozens of SoC’s and they typically takes 3 to 5 years just to design and need to be able to run for the next 20 years. In contrast, the mobile SoC’s with Android support are very powerful, but they are only produced for 1 to 3 years.

If the designers of Linux phones want to be able to run a Linux kernel (as opposed to an Android kernel), most of their options offer little processing power, poor energy efficiency and poor thermals. If they want to avoid proprietary blobs as much as possible, they either have to choose an older SoC which the community has been worked on for years to iron out the bugs in the free/open source drivers (e.g., the Allwinner A64 in the PinePhone), or they have to spend years patching the Linux kernel to properly support a newer SoC (as Purism did with the NXP i.MX 8M Quad in the Librem 5). If they decide that it is too much trouble to send their patches to mainline Linux, then it will be harder to support their hardware in the future, because they will have to keep applying those same patches to each new Linux kernel release. Purism chose to upstream their changes to mainline Linux, whereas the volunteers working on the PinePhone’s kernel generally didn’t.

The chipmakers like NXP, Rockchip, Allwinner, Amlogic and Broadcom who make SoC’s with Linux support simply don’t generate enough revenue to justify using a leading-edge process node in their chips. Even thought they can sell a Linux SoC for 10 to 15 years, they still won’t sell enough to recoup the extra $400 – $600 million that it would cost to use a 3nm process, and the costs keep going up with each new generation of process node. It is enormously expensive to design a chip at the cutting edge of the silicon industry. Silicon Analysts estimates that fabbing a wafer at TSMC costs an average of $19,500 at a 3nm node, but only $3000 at a 28nm node. Most Linux SoC’s are low-priced chips with very thin margins, so they have to sell millions of them to make much profit. Some Linux SoC makers like NXP focus on specialty markets, so they they have higher margins, but they tend not to sell many of them, so the profits are still limited.

Another way that chipmakers reduce their costs is by using older generations of Cortex cores, which have lower licensing fees from ARM, and by using lots of low-power Cortex-A5 series cores, rather than the higher-power Cortex-A7 and Cortex-X series cores, which have higher licensing fees. NXP was clearly employing these cost cutting strategies with its i.MX 9 series processors. The most powerful chip in the series is the i.MX 95, which contains six Cortex-A55 cores, when it would have made more sense to use two Cortex-A78 cores for better performance and four Cortex-A55 cores for low power. The i.MX 95 was fabbed on a 16nm FinFET node, which is a slight regression compared to Samsung’s 14nm node used in the previous i.MX 8M Plus processor. Another example of this cost cutting was the decision by Broadcom to use older Cortex cores and larger process nodes in its BCM2836 (40nm, 4x Cortex-A7), BCM2837 (40nm, 4x Cortex-A53), BCM2711 (28nm, 4x Cortex-A72) and BCM2712 (16nm, 4x Cortex-A76), despite knowing that millions of these chips would be sold (e.g. in the Raspberry Pi 2, 3, 4 and 5, respectively). Even Nvidia, which is the world’s richest chipmaker due to the AI boom, appears to be avoiding the costs of using a leading-edge process node in its Tegra SoC. It canceled its planned Tegra Atlan series which was announced in April 2021, and still hasn’t released its Tegra Thor series, which was announced in September 2022. Its Tegra Orin series which was announced in March 2018 is now getting long in the tooth using an 8nm node.

In contrast, the smartphone market, which sold 1.25 billion units in 2025, has enormous volumes, and phone makers are willing to pay much higher prices for processors, especially for higher-end models, which allows Qualcomm, MediaTek, Apple, Samsung, Google, Xiaomi and Huawei to make the enormous investment in designing mobile SoC’s for an expensive 3nm process node. Integrated mobile SoC’s such as the Qualcomm Snapdragon and MediaTek Dimensity have so much volume, that they can make a profit despite the fact that they generally are only produced for 2 to 3 years. The flagship models (e.g. the Snapdragon 8 series and Dimensity 9000 series) lose most of their market after the first year, so SoC makers have to charge much higher prices to cover the enormous costs of using a leading-edge process node.

In addition, the integrated mobile SoC’s that run Android incorporate many additional functions, such as WiFi, Bluetooth, cellular modem, GPS/GNSS, audio DAC, charge controller and USB-C controller, which require separate chips on Linux phones like the PinePhone and Librem 5. This makes the circuit boards for these phones much more expensive to design, because they can’t just take a standard reference design from Qualcomm or MediaTek and tweak it. They have to custom design their circuit boards, which requires two to three times more components than the circuit board for a typical smartphone. The Librem 5 contains 74 integrated circuits (IC’s) and its 4 circuit boards have a total of 1267 components, making it the most complex phone ever created. The six chips (i.MX 8M Quad, RS9116, BM818, Teseo-LIV3F, WM8962 and bq25895) that the Librem 5 uses in place of an integrated mobile SoC occupy a total of 1483.2 mm2, which is 10 times the area of a Snapdragon 8. Purism had to use components as small as 0201 and use 10 layers to cram everything into the Librem 5’s main circuit board, which had to go through roughly a dozen iterations to get right. Even a minimal Linux phone like the PinePhone, which was designed to be as economical as possible, has 24 ICs and a total of 627 components on its circuit board, making it more complex than most Android smartphones, which typically have 300 to 400 components.

Adding all these extra components makes Linux phones more expensive to design and produce, but it also makes them suck more power, since it takes more energy to run half a dozen chips at larger node sizes than one integrated mobile SoC fabbed on a ≤4nm node, which has been optimized for energy efficiency. It is hard to coordinate all the extra chips in Linux phones to get them to enter lower power modes and sleep modes to conserve their batteries. Add in an SoC with an older node size between 28 and 8 nm that hasn’t been optimized for battery usage, and there is no way that Linux phones can match the battery life of Android phones.

The RK3588S changes the game for Linux phones

Many of the Linux SoC makers have decided that they shouldn’t even bother building chips with the CPU and GPU performance needed for a modern computing device. NXP, Amlogic and Broadcom simply aren’t interested in building competitive SoC’s. However, Rockchip fundamentally changed the prospects for making a decent Linux phone when it released the RK3588 and its lower-power RK3588S variant in 2022. With four 2.4Ghz Cortex-A76 cores and four 1.8GHz Cortex-A55 cores and a Mali-G610 MC4 graphics, there finally was a Linux SoC on the market with enough processing power in an SoC to make an acceptable Linux smartphone.

The Liberux NEXX and the Dawndrums Divine D. are two Linux phones currently being designed with the RK3588S. Purism just announced on its forum that its next gen phone “is actively being designed,” which won’t be based on the i.MX platform like the Librem 5 and Liberty, so it is likely to be based on the RK3588S as well.

Of course, the RK3588S’s performance isn’t going to impress anyone used to an Android phone. The RK3588 and RK3588S are fabbed on a 8nm process node, which is four generations behind today’s state of the art at 2nm. Today’s low-end Snapdragon 4 Gen 4 fabbed at 4nm has 25% better single core CPU performance than the RK3588. (See the table of SoC’s below.) The RK3588S has similar performance to the RK3588 over short bursts, but its performance is 10%-15% less over periods longer than 30 seconds. By the time the Linux phones based on the RK3588S hit the market in a couple years time, they will have significantly worse performance than every Android smartphone on the market, but this difference probably won’t be a limiting factor for most people who aren’t 3D gaming or shooting high-resolution video. At a certain point, more processing power has diminishing utility, and the RK3588S crosses that threshold for most people.

Where the older process tech will be significant is in terms of their energy consumption. A phone sporting the RK3588S will run hotter and have worse battery life than phones using integrated mobile SoC’s from Qualcomm, MediaTek, UNISOC, etc. The challenge is to dissipate the extra heat. The PinePhone’s SoC and cellular modem get very hot because they do not have a good heat spreader. The Librem 5’s SoC is joined with thermal paste to the back plate of the LCD, which avoids overheating the SoC, but causes the touchscreen and aluminum frame to become hot to the touch. A Linux phone with the RK3358S will need some kind of heat dissipation, but it is unlikely that a Linux phone maker can afford to include a vapor chamber, since those have to be specially designed and would be very expensive for a phone of limited production. The RK3588S will need throttling to fit within the power and thermal envelope of a phone.

At first, the mainline Linux support for the RK3588 was very limited. Rockchip never published the documentation for the chip on its opensource.rock-chips.com site, and Rockchip took down that site at the end of 2025. On December 21, 2024, Jeff Geerling, the most influential reviewer in the Raspberry Pi alternative space, reported on X:

I was told Rockchip no longer considers RK3588 to be an ‘open source’ chip. In practice, that means Rockchip no longer contributes RK3588 code to open-source projects and they also decided not to sell RK3588 directly to SBC vendors.

On the other hand, Rockchip is still updating its public kernel code for the RK3588 at Github. Collabora has been toiling away for years to add mainline Linux support for the RK3588, and Collabora lists Rockchip as one of its partners, so Rockchip hasn’t totally abandoned the Linux community. Collabora recently added mainline Linux support for the video capture unit and MIPI CSI2 interface in the RK3588. Now Collabora is working with Ideas on Board and Rockchip to create an new image signal processor (ISP) driver for all the RK35xx chips. Collabora has gotten the hardware video decoder working for VP8, VP9, H.264 and H.265, and is now working on the hardware video encoder for H.264.

Four years after the RK3588S was released, there is now enough support for the chip in mainline Linux, that an RK3588S phone can be created running free/open source drivers. The RK3588S needs to execute a proprietary Synopsys DDR timing training blob at boot. The RK3588S can now use the free Panthor driver for the GPU, but it requires the proprietary mali_csffw.bin firmware, which is loaded from the /lib/firmware/ directory at boot. All the other blobs for the RK3588S have either been replaced or they aren’t necessary. There is now a FOSS driver for the NPU. The proprietary MPP library for hardware video encoding can be replaced with software video encoding, and Collabora is working on free drivers for the ISP and hardware video encode, so software video processing and encoding probably won’t be necessary in the future. Collabora has developed a FOSS version for the Trusted Firmware-A (TF-A), that can replace the proprietary bl31.elf firmware. The HDMI blob in the bl31.elf isn’t necessary if using DisplayPort Alt Mode for video out. The proprietary bl32.bin firmware is not necessary if not using the hardware-enforced security features or encryption keys.

If Purism uses the RK3588S in its next phone, it is unlikely that Purism will attempt to obtain the FSF’s RYF certification by storing blobs on a separate NOR Flash chip and executing them with a secondary processor, like it did with the Librem 5, because the RK3588S doesn’t have a secondary low-power processor like the Cortex-M4 in the i.MX 8M Quad.

There are good prospects that Collabora will eventually get all the RK3588S’s hardware supported with mainline Linux drivers, and until then, the RK3588S has enough processing power in the GPU and CPU to process and encode images and video in software, so a phone built on the RK3588S can take photos and shoot video like a normal smartphone, although it will be slower and use more energy. In contrast, previous phones using a Linux kernel simply didn’t have enough processing power to offer decent cameras. Taking photos on the PinePhone is painfully slow, and the Millipixels app on the Librem 5 pauses for 3 to 4 seconds after taking each photo. Because the Librem 5’s i.MX 8M Quad processor lacks an ISP, audio DSP and hardware video encoder, it takes roughly 10 times as long to process and encode a 720p video on the Librem 5 as the length of the video, and it’s about 50 times longer on the PinePhone, due to its antiquated Allwinner A64 processor and significantly slower RAM and eMMC.

Aside from the RK3588S, there aren’t many other options on the market to make a good Linux phone. The closest option is Nvidia’s Tegra Orin NX or Orin Nano Super, which only contain Cortex-A78 cores. They lack low-power Cortex-A55 cores and they aren’t very energy efficient. They sport newer tech than the Cortex-A76 cores in the RK3588S, but the Tegra Orin was fabbed on a 8nm node just like the RK3588S and the Tegra Orin’s lower clock rate limits its single-core performance. The Tegra Orin is also much worse in terms of software freedom, requiring proprietary blobs in the initial bootloader, the secondary bootloader, Boot Configuration Table, USB firmware, HDMI/DisplayPort configuration, ARM Trusted Firmware and the GPU userspace driver. Many Linux users can ignore the blobs in the Linux device tree, but it harder to ignore Nvidia’s GPU blob in the userspace.

Another option is the Allwinner A733 at 12nm, which offers less performance with dual 2.0Ghz Cortex-A76 cores and six 1.79GHz Cortex-A55 low-power cores. Allwinner stopped collaborating with the Linux community years ago, and has frequently violated the GPL 2.0 license by not publishing its Linux source code, so the A733 probably won’t be a viable option, although Allwinner has made some comments about intending to collaborate with the community in the future. Another option is the NXP i.MX 95, which does have mainline Linux support, but its performance on a 16nm node with six low-power Cortex-A55 cores is poor and not a good value for the money.

The PinePhone 2 was expected to use the more economical Rockchip RK3566, which is fabbed on an older 22nm node. However, so many volunteer developers abandoned PINE64 in 2022, that it is unlikely that the Hong Kong-based company will be launching any new models in the foreseeable future. The production of PINE64’s Linux devices are currently on hold due to the high RAM and eMMC prices caused by the AI boom, but part of the reason is that PINE64 can’t raise prices on many of its devices, which are only half-functional, because many of the community groups, who worked on the PinePhone, are no longer developing the software to support PINE64’s newer devices.
