The Mineral That Built Civilization

The history of salt begins with some of the world’s earliest civilizations. By around 3,000 BCE, salt was being harvested in China and Egypt. By 252 BCE, workers in China were drilling brine wells more than 300 feet deep, and by 200 AD, China was using

natural gas

to process salt.

Salt’s influence even shows up in language. Words like salary, salad, sausage, and sauce can all be traced back to salt, showing how deeply the mineral became embedded in daily life.

From Preservation to Infrastructure

For centuries, salt’s most important role was food preservation. Salted meat and fish helped feed armies, support long voyages, and expand trade across continents.

But salt’s role changed as technology evolved. Canning and refrigeration reduced its dominance in food preservation, while modern chemistry, energy, and infrastructure created new uses.

Today, salt is used in thousands of applications. Its most visible modern market is road de-icing, where governments and transportation agencies rely on it to keep

highway driving

safer during winter.

Salt’s Enduring Demand

Salt has been in demand for 5,000 years and has more than 14,000 documented uses. Today, it remains essential to North American road de-icing, a recurring, government-funded infrastructure market with few scalable substitutes.
